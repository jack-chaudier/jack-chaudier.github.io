Memo · product & data journalism

Data-journalism research platform roadmap

Jack Gaffney

July 19, 2026 · 7 min read · Data ingestion, tooling, and editorial strategy

Abstract

To maximize its utility as a data-journalism tool, the platform has to grow from a robust search engine into an investigative workbench — one that can trace the full lifecycle of government action from proposal to result. The existing feature set already solves the hard problem: finding high-quality primary sources without needing to know in advance how a corpus is classified. Semantic querying is the cornerstone of that strength. Even with only a rough understanding of which documents might be relevant, a reporter can quickly assemble an authoritative list of places to start. What's missing is context that doesn't stop at the document — context that shows how sources relate, and lets a reporter test whether a policy produced the effect it promised.

Recommends an authority-and-status layer · verification in the synthesis layer · a persistent workspace · four datasets to ingest first

The evaluation

I built the evaluation around one case study: could the platform support a report on whether federal tax incentives for electric vehicles deliver different value depending on where a consumer lives? A semantic search surfaced a 2010 EV hearing, 2023 congressional testimony, EPA and NHTSA rulemakings, and the governing statutory and regulatory provisions. Exact searches for 26 U.S.C. § 30D and 40 C.F.R. § 600.116-12 then resolved to codified text and canonical sources.

That pipeline is where the platform shines: a broad question in, an exact result out. It is also exactly where the functionality could be expanded so that nuanced arguments can be made more quickly.

Tools to build

An authority-and-status layer

The highest-priority addition is a way to see what a document actually is. One of my first semantic results was titled "Electric Vehicle Consumer Incentive Tax Act." At first glance the title sounded like enacted legislation. On investigation it was an Extension of Remarks from March 15, 1999, in which a member announced a proposal. If the legislative path that Act took were available on the page, a reporter could diagnose that in seconds and use it. Even a negative result — a proposal that stalled — is a valuable result. The more of those a reporter can see in one place, the more completely they understand a document's real bearing on the story.

In an ideal world a reporter never has to deal with these complications, and the platform bridges the gap. Connecting a congressional statement to the corresponding bill, committee actions, subsequent versions, enacted descendants, and current codified provisions would all be significant additions as native features. Writers are the primary stakeholders here. Every second saved directly increases the platform's utility to them — and as its usefulness increases, broader adoption naturally accelerates the system's overall maturity.

Verification in the synthesis layer

The second layer of the platform, AI report creation, has the potential to be the real time saver. Today it does a good job sifting the top connected results and presenting them in a form that is easy to read and gets to the point. The room for improvement is in claim verification. The reports are synthetic and can never be perfectly reliable, but some steps clearly move in that direction.

Two findings from testing. In one query, the generated report described the new, used, and commercial clean-vehicle credits as available "today," relying on 2023 and 2024 agency descriptions — although the current statutes terminate those credits for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. In another, the platform retrieved NHTSA's December 2025 SAFE Vehicles Rule III proposal, 90 Fed. Reg. 56,438, but concluded that no subsequent revision changed the treatment of the credits. The proposal itself says NHTSA considered their effects de minimis under the updated constraints and removed their availability from its analysis.

Neither of these is an argument against the synthesis layer. They are the kind of insight more user testing will keep producing. If the reports pulled more context from the full text of the most semantically relevant documents and showed a sharper summary of it, users would spend less time running the specific-search function against a document that turns out not to be relevant at all.

A persistent workspace

The broader recommendation is a dedicated, persistent workspace. Letting writers save documents, synthesized reports, and even the queries that produced them would raise the volume of searching a person can sustain, because a reporter could pick up where they left off when a research route didn't pan out. That turns the platform from a place where research is found into a place where a story is planned and executed.

Data to prioritize

Looking through the lens of my use case, the most valuable additions are the datasets that supply the second layer of information — the ones I had to go to myself, all of which have public APIs. The platform already gives me the federal and lawmaking side; the efficiency of my research would have increased dramatically if I could have cross-referenced climate, efficiency, and IRS data in the same place. Good stories take an overarching angle and then strengthen it by looking further. The ability for broad data to be augmented by specific data is very powerful.

Ingestion priorities from the EV case study. Priority reflects how directly each source strengthens a primary-source argument.
Source What it adds Priority
IRS credit claims by state and income level Who actually claimed the credit, and where — the difference between a policy on paper and a policy in effect. High
Department of Energy: registrations, chargers, state incentives The physical and policy landscape a federal incentive lands in. High
EPA and National Renewable Energy Laboratory testing results Efficiency and climate variation — the mechanism by which a blanket policy produces uneven value. High
Regulations.gov comments and attachments Who tried to shape a rule, in their own words. Medium
Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs review records Where a rule slowed down or changed inside the executive branch. Medium
State legislation, regulations, and public-utility commission dockets The state-level counterpart to federal action — the connection between congressional records and outcomes on the ground. Medium
Police reports, journalism archives Big-picture color, but a risk to the primary-source foundation: they can introduce unintentional bias. Low

The medium-priority sources all have APIs and all serve the platform's original story: connecting congressional records to results in the states. The low-priority ones are valuable for big-picture understanding, but they could undercut the primary-source foundation that makes the tool trustworthy in the first place.

Stories to develop

My story — a non-partisan look at EV tax incentives — is, to my mind, what this was built for. There is a layer beneath the front-facing appearance: one that looks for unavoidable differences in climate that complicate the fairness of a blanket policy. The platform made that natural, and with the additions above the process would have been faster still.

The benefit of a semantic database system like this is the one I started with: it separates technical knowledge of a corpus from understanding of a topic. I am not an expert in tax law, but a service like this gives me confidence that natural-language queries can collect primary sources efficiently. That is how it becomes valuable to anyone writing evidence-backed, non-partisan reports. More of the effort goes into the report itself, and less into hunting for the specific proposed bill buried in a database where keyword search is the only way in.

Written after a structured evaluation of the platform against a single reporting task. Citations are to the documents the platform returned during that evaluation.
